# Vegetarian and vegan food in Japan

## Hidden ingredients

- Stocks and sauces often contain meat or fish, so a dish can look plant-based and still not be.
- **Dashi** (fish stock) and **bonito flakes** are the most common hidden ingredients. Even the sauce packed with natto often contains dashi.
- In Japan "vegetarian" is understood loosely. Saying you're vegetarian may not be enough; say exactly what you don't eat.

## Usually easier dishes (still check the sauce or broth)

- Rice, noodles and tofu in many forms; miso and natto.
- Vegetable tempura, pickles, miso-grilled aubergine, cold soba (zaru soba).
- Sushi rolls with cucumber or pickled daikon, and inarizushi (rice in fried tofu pouches).
- Convenience stores: umeboshi or kombu onigiri, edamame, packaged beans, simple salads.
- Vegetarian okonomiyaki sometimes exists, but it contains egg, so it is not vegan.

## Shojin ryori (temple cuisine)

- Buddhist temple cuisine, mostly served at temples. It's a practical base for meals only if you stay in temple lodging (shukubo).
- Traditionally vegan and avoids strong flavours such as garlic and onion. Dairy is sometimes used, so ask ahead.

## Finding places

- Look for vegan or vegetarian stickers from the Japan Vegan Society, VegeProject Japan or the Japan Vegetarian Association.
- Directories JNTO mentions: HappyCow, Vegewel, Japan VegeMap.
- In rural areas, ryokan can often prepare vegetarian meals if you ask in advance.

## Ordering tips

- Be specific: no meat, seafood or animal products, **including dashi and stock**. If staff seem unsure, explain that "no meat" also means chicken, pork and fish.
- Carry a written card in Japanese listing your restrictions, and mention your diet when you book, especially for set menus.
